If you've ever finished a workout at a CrossFit competition and then spent
ten minutes trying to work out where you actually placed, you're not alone.
Competition scoring looks simple from the outside — fastest time or most
reps wins — but the details around time caps and tiebreakers trip up a lot
of first-time competitors. Here's how it actually works.

## Reps vs. time: know which one you're being scored on

Most CrossFit workouts score one of two ways:

- **For time** — complete the full prescribed work as fast as possible.
  Whoever finishes first, wins that workout.
- **For reps (AMRAP)** — complete as many rounds and reps as possible in a
  fixed time window. Whoever completes the most work, wins.

Some workouts combine both: a for-time workout with a time cap, where
athletes who don't finish are scored on reps completed instead of time.

## Time caps: what happens if you don't finish

A time cap is the maximum time allowed for a for-time workout. If you don't
finish within the cap, you don't get a "did not finish" — you get scored on
how far you got. Typically that means: total reps completed, or reps
completed *at* the cap plus a fraction for partial movements, depending on
how the workout is written. Always read the workout description before you
start, not after you hit the cap — it tells you exactly how partial reps
are counted.

## How overall placement is calculated

Multi-workout competitions almost always use **point-based overall
scoring**, not combined times. Each workout is scored independently — first
place in that workout gets 1 point (or the top score gets 100 points, worked
down), second gets 2, and so on. Your overall placement is the sum of your
points across all workouts, lowest total wins. This is why a strong finish
in the final workout can still move you up the leaderboard even if you
didn't win it outright — it's about consistent placement across every
event, not raw scores.

## Tiebreakers: the part everyone forgets to check

When two athletes have the same overall points total, competitions use a
tiebreaker — usually the athlete's placement in the final workout, or a
head-to-head count of who beat whom more often across all workouts. Some
competitions specify a dedicated tiebreak workout instead. Check the
competition's rules before day one so a tie doesn't come as a surprise.

## Why this matters when you're picking events

Scoring format changes how you should pace a competition. If it's pure
for-time with no cap, banking energy for a fast finish matters less than
just finishing efficiently. If it's points-based across multiple workouts,
a mediocre finish in one workout is recoverable — but a "did not finish"
usually isn't, since most scoring systems rank a DNF last regardless of
partial reps.
